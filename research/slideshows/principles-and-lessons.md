# Slideshow principles and lessons

Source notes from three X articles about TikTok slideshow marketing.
The headings and short examples retain the authors' concepts and phrasing where useful; the explanations are summaries.
Claims, revenue figures, conversion figures, and algorithm explanations have not been independently verified.

## Sources

- Pounds, [Direct-response TikTok slideshow course](https://x.com/pounddz/status/2095531593003606066?s=20)
- Sulfur, [Slideshow beginner playbook and production system](https://x.com/sulfurscales/status/2088703056536109114?s=20)
- Pedro Pérez, [AI UGC slideshow workflow](https://x.com/PerezHatesAI/status/2098064168058241089?s=20)

## Shared philosophy

All three authors recommend the same broad loop:

1. Find recent content that has already demonstrated demand.
2. Analyze why people stopped, continued, and acted.
3. Reuse the underlying mechanism without copying the original post.
4. Produce several distinct variations.
5. Publish enough tests to identify repeated results.
6. Turn winners into templates.
7. Treat losers as information.

They do not recommend asking an AI model to invent a complete strategy without real examples.

## Pounds: direct-response lessons

### Treat slides as direct response, not cheap traffic

Pounds criticizes the "copy and spam" approach.
He argues that a slideshow should capture attention, develop desire, and convert a specifically selected audience.

### Blend into the feed

His comparison is Gary Halbert's distinction between personal-looking mail that gets opened and obvious advertising that gets discarded.
Applied to TikTok: if the post looks like an advertisement, viewers are more likely to swipe away.

### Use pattern interrupts

Halbert attached coins or money to letters to force attention.
Pounds says the slideshow equivalent is an unusual image, statement, or detail on the first slide.

### Target one specific person

His lesson is: "Don't make TikToks for everyone."
The account and post should be personalized around one person, problem, and desire.

Example:

- "broke college girl who still has acne"

### Relevance beats reach

Pounds says a smaller number of highly targeted views can be worth more than a much larger number of random views.
The objective is conversion rather than vanity metrics.

### Simple often beats polished

He recounts Halbert producing a glossy magazine that barely broke even.
The lesson is that more polish and more information do not necessarily produce better results.
A slideshow can win by being native, clear, simple, and focused on one idea.

### Sell what people already want

Pounds says not to manufacture desire.
Research the audience's existing pain, fear, or ambition and position the product around it.

### Sell the transformation, not the ingredient

His Halbert example contrasts marketing a cactus-based cosmetic by its ingredients with calling it "A Face Lift In a Jar."
The transformation is easier to understand and closer to the buyer's desire.

### Research creates the hook

Pounds says Halbert spent far longer determining what to say than writing the final advertisement.
Deep audience and product research should reveal the angle and "Big Idea."
Clever wording cannot rescue an average idea.

### Slide one is everything

Attention is the first stage of AIDA.
If the first slide does not stop the viewer, none of the later information can matter.

### Every slide earns the next swipe

The job of each slide is to create enough interest for the next slide.
The sequence should maintain curiosity rather than merely labeling sections.

### One slideshow equals one core desire

Do not overload one sequence with several products, benefits, or ideas.
Keep the desire and action clear.

### Build an owned audience

Pounds recommends using traffic to build email, community, or another audience that the marketer controls instead of depending entirely on one platform.
The following communication should keep providing value and connect to an aligned offer.

## Sulfur: distribution and production lessons

### Slideshows create dwell time, saves, and completion

Sulfur believes people reading at their own speed, moving backward through slides, saving useful information, and finishing short sequences provide strong platform signals.
This is his explanation rather than confirmed TikTok documentation.

### Text changes the viewing state

He argues that video often places people in an entertainment state while reading places them in an information-processing state, which may help product communication.

### Volume with a quality floor

Sulfur says one excellent video per week may lose to a larger number of adequate slideshows.
His reason for favoring slideshows is that they make sustained volume possible for one person.

### Narrow relatability matters more than broad attention

Content must select for the person who would want the product.
A mismatch between content audience and product audience can produce views without sales or installs.

### Steal the recipe, not the slides

Study several outperforming posts to identify their shared structure.
Do not reuse another creator's actual slides.
One reference encourages imitation; several references reveal the underlying pattern.

### Recent repeated performance matters

A single viral post can reflect timing or luck.
A format working repeatedly, recently, and across related accounts is stronger evidence.

### Reference images improve generated output

Sulfur recommends showing an image model the desired visual style instead of relying only on written style descriptions.
He also recommends generating one image at a time and providing scene context incrementally.

### Real environments can improve credibility

One technique he gives is photographing a real environment and asking an image model to place the product into it.
He believes real surroundings combined with generated elements can feel more authentic than a fully generated scene.

### Do not make everything AI

Sulfur says fully generated slideshows can feel synthetic.
He recommends mixing generated assets with real imagery and maintaining one visual tone.
Images still require permission or an appropriate license.

### Native text can appear more organic

For pain funnels and swaps, he prefers adding text with TikTok's own text tool.
He reserves designed, baked-in typography mainly for formats where visual design is central to the identity.

### Disclose generated content

Sulfur advises creators not to fight TikTok's AI label or hide generated imagery.
He says a strong hook matters more and hiding generated content creates unnecessary account risk.

### Transfer proven ideas across media

Instead of looking only for viral slideshows, he recommends turning proven video mechanisms into slideshow structures.
This expands the set of validated ideas without copying the original media.

### Treat winners like a mining claim

When a format works, produce many fresh versions until the result decays.
Do not abandon a working mechanism merely because it has already been used once.

### The portfolio is the machine

Sulfur says most posts may receive little distribution and a small minority may drive most results.
His answer is a portfolio of posts and accounts rather than expecting every post to succeed.
The numerical hit rates in the article are anecdotal.

### Make viewers feel they found the product

His key distinction is between announcing a product and letting a viewer notice it through use, a screenshot, or a result.
The viewer asking for the product name signals stronger intent than passively receiving a direct pitch.

### Build for the screenshot

He recommends designing products with at least one screen, widget, result, or visual moment that is understandable in under a second.
The product should be demonstrable inside the content format.

### Start local, then go global

Sulfur reports that one operator found it easier to learn and gain reach in a native market than through a VPN-targeted US account.
His proposed order is to validate a format naturally, then adapt a proven winner for harder or more valuable markets.

### Search, then repeat

During early testing, try several formats.
Once one crosses a meaningful threshold, switch from searching to repeating that structure.
If no format works after varied testing, reconsider the offer or audience rather than changing only surface creative.

### Hooks and images matter more than minor posting details

Sulfur treats music, hashtags, and captions as secondary.
Music should match the emotion and captions should remain coherent and searchable, but neither compensates for a weak hook or opening image.

### Automation removes grunt work, not human judgment

His compromise between manual and scaled production is to automate asset production and scheduling while preserving human review and native final touches.

## Pedro Pérez: operational lessons

### Do not start from complete originality

Pedro begins with current successful formats.
He seeks repeated evidence rather than assuming a new idea will work because it sounds creative.

### Analyze the mechanism, not only the topic

He studies the hook, storyline, emotion, pacing, visual progression, payoff, call to action, and comments.
The goal is to explain why someone stopped and kept swiping.

### Comments are research

Highly liked comments show what viewers noticed, questioned, or emotionally connected with.
Pedro treats this as part of the post analysis rather than an afterthought.

### AI needs real examples

His prompt asks AI to study several demonstrated winners and extract a reusable framework.
He rejects generic requests such as "Give me 20 viral TikTok ideas" because they tend to produce ideas untethered to evidence.

### Keep the psychological structure, change the scenario

The useful reusable element is the mechanism—such as suspicion followed by discovery—not the original subject or wording.
New concepts should preserve the mechanism while changing relationships, setting, images, and details.

### Batch production

Pedro generates approximately 10 to 15 slideshows in one session so the week's production is completed together.
The objective is a repeatable weekly system, not spending all day perfecting one post.

### AI does not replace the difficult part

Research, selection, interpretation, and creative judgment happen before scheduling.
The agent handles organization and repetitive distribution work after the content strategy has been decided.

### Winners become templates; losers become data

Pedro repeats formats exceeding his performance threshold and stops formats that remain weak.
His personal thresholds are approximately 10,000–15,000 views for repeating and below 3,000 for stopping.
Those numbers are not universal and should be adapted to account baseline and product results.

## Important cautions not resolved by the articles

- Algorithm explanations are observations and hypotheses, not official proof.
- Revenue, view, and conversion examples are not independently verified.
- Reusing a format does not authorize copying images, wording, characters, or other protected creative work.
- Pinterest availability does not mean an image is licensed for commercial reuse.
- AI-generated people and stories must not be presented as genuine customer evidence.
- Generated-content disclosures and platform policies still apply.
- VPN, purchased-account, location-spoofing, and aggressive automation methods are fragile and may violate platform rules.
- Views should not substitute for installs, activation, retention, or revenue when evaluating marketing effectiveness.
