# Slideshow hook patterns

Source notes from three X articles about TikTok slideshow marketing. The short examples below preserve the authors' phrasing where useful; the surrounding explanations are summaries. Claims and performance figures belong to the authors and have not been independently verified.

## Sources

-   Pounds, [Direct-response TikTok slideshow course](https://x.com/pounddz/status/2095531593003606066?s=20)

## Pounds: direct-response headline patterns

Pounds connects slideshow hooks to Gary Halbert's direct-response methods and AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action.

### News

Frame the opening as something newly discovered, newly available, or finally solved.

Examples given:

-   "Something new"
    
-   "Scientific discovery"
    
-   "At last"
    

### Promise a benefit

Tell viewers what they will get from continuing through the slideshow.

### Combine news and benefit

Present a new discovery together with the result it promises.

### Specificity

Use exact numbers or details to make the statement feel concrete and authoritative.

Example given:

-   "How to lose 13 LB in 17 days"
    

### Warning

Tell the viewer not to take an action until another condition has been met.

Template given:

-   "Don't do \_ until you do \_"
    

### Secrets

Promise access to information that appears hidden or not widely known.

### Numbered list

Use a specific quantity to make the value and length immediately understandable.

Template given:

-   "17 ways to \_"
    

### Specific-person personalization

Address one narrow person, problem, and desire instead of speaking to everybody.

Example given:

-   "broke college girl who still has acne"
    

### Transformation instead of ingredient

Name the outcome people already want rather than leading with the mechanism or ingredients.

Examples given:

-   "A Face Lift In a Jar"
    
-   Instead of "Best supplements to take for gym beginners": "Supplements that will make you feel like you're on steroids"
    
-   Instead of "Best ways to lose fat fast": "These 3 things made me drop fat around my belly without even trying"
    

### Pattern interrupt

Use an unusual opening image, object, statement, or visual detail that interrupts habitual scrolling. Pounds relates this to Halbert attaching coins or money to sales letters to capture attention.

## Sulfur: standard first-slide formulas

### Numbered promise

Example given:

-   "7 things that fixed my gut"
    

### Signs and symptoms

Example given:

-   "3 signs your pores need a deep clean"
    

### Secret knowledge

Example given:

-   "this feels illegal to know"
    

### Relatable pain

Example given:

-   "why does my flat look like a student loan"
    

### Another person, conflict or doubt, then a changed reaction

Sulfur describes this as a stronger mechanism than opening with the product itself. The involvement of another person creates story and tension before the result appears.

Examples discussed:

-   "my landlord said i can't change anything so i showed her what AI thinks it could look like"
    
-   "i showed my mum what AI thinks our living room could be"
    
-   "babe why do you have an umbrella in your car"
    

### Curiosity gap

Open a loop that can only be resolved by swiping. Sulfur says relationship drama often uses this mechanism.

Examples given:

-   "we were together six months, then he sent me this"
    
-   "i found out what he'd been hiding"
    

### Borrowed credibility

Combine an open loop with proof, status, or a result that gives the viewer a reason to believe.

Examples given:

-   "extremely chic things my twin sister does"
    
-   "how i kept a 4.5 gpa with adhd"
    

### Cultural mystery

Promise inside information from a distant, unfamiliar, or hidden world.

Examples given:

-   "gym rules in japan that will shock westerners"
    
-   "secret codes at chinese business lunches that decide if you get the deal"
    

### Information as the value

Make the usefulness of the information itself the reason to swipe. Sulfur notes that the following slides must deliver substance because suspense is doing less work.

Examples given:

-   "top 10 degrees that make you rich"
    
-   "my clean iphone setup"
    

### Repeatable signature

Use a recognizable opening repeatedly until the repetition becomes part of the account's identity.

Example given:

-   "hey, i'm joe, you doomscrolled your way to my art, enjoy"
    

### Engagement bait

Ask a question designed mainly to produce comments, such as asking viewers what they would place in an empty room. Sulfur says it may work occasionally but warns that depending on it can lead to suppression.

## Sulfur: image and slide-level hooks

Sulfur says the hook is both the words and the image. He gives an example where the same story received very different results when a generic image was replaced by a close-up crying face. His conclusion is that emotion must be legible at thumbnail size and that the pain or result should be visible immediately.

He also says every slide needs its own hook. A label such as "step 2" gives little reason to continue, while a line promising a surprising or stronger result keeps the sequence open.

Example given:

-   "step 2: the trick that feels harder but works 3x faster"
    

## Pedro Pérez: hook-analysis criteria

Pedro does not provide a separate taxonomy. He studies recent successful posts and asks what made viewers stop and continue. For every reference, he records:

-   The first one or two seconds.
    
-   The hook.
    
-   The first image.
    
-   The story.
    
-   The curiosity mechanism.
    
-   The emotional trigger.
    
-   The visual progression.
    
-   The amount of text.
    
-   The pacing.
    
-   The payoff.
    
-   The call to action.
    
-   The comments with the most likes.
    

His example of an underlying mechanism is a story beginning with suspected cheating. He says the useful pattern is not "cheating" itself; it is suspicion, missing information, and a later reveal. He gives these alternative openings to show how the mechanism can move to another scenario:

-   "I thought my best friend was hiding something from me. Then I saw this photo."
    
-   "My boss called me into his office after work. I had no idea why until he showed me this."
    
-   "My girlfriend told me she was going to sleep early. At 2:14 AM, I received this message."
