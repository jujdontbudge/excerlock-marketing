# Slideshow articles: business and production lessons

The lessons from three X articles on TikTok slideshow marketing that are not about writing a deck: how to research, produce, test, scale and monetise. The deck-writing lessons (hooks, formats and the don'ts) live in `sections/slideshows/skills/hooks-and-formats/`.

The explanations are summaries. Revenue, view and conversion figures and algorithm explanations are the authors' own and were not independently verified.

## Sources

- Pounds, [Direct-response TikTok slideshow course](https://x.com/pounddz/status/2095531593003606066?s=20). Direct response in the style of Gary Halbert.
- Sulfur, [Slideshow beginner playbook and production system](https://x.com/sulfurscales/status/2088703056536109114?s=20).
- Pedro Pérez, [AI UGC slideshow workflow](https://x.com/PerezHatesAI/status/2098064168058241089?s=20).

## The shared loop

All three authors run the same loop:

1. Find recent content that has already shown demand.
2. Work out why people stopped, kept going and acted.
3. Reuse the underlying mechanism.
4. Produce several distinct variations.
5. Publish enough tests to see repeated results.
6. Turn winners into templates.
7. Treat losers as information.

None of them asks an AI model to invent a strategy without real examples.

## Research

- **Start from what already works, not from originality (Pedro Pérez).** He begins with current successful formats and wants repeated evidence, not an idea that merely sounds creative.
- **Analyse the mechanism, not only the topic (Pedro Pérez).** He studies the hook, storyline, emotion, pacing, visual progression, payoff, call to action and comments to explain why someone stopped and kept swiping.
- **Comments are research (Pedro Pérez).** The most-liked comments show what viewers noticed, questioned or felt. They are part of the analysis, not an afterthought.
- **AI needs real examples (Pedro Pérez).** His prompt gives the AI several proven winners and asks it to pull out a reusable framework. Generic requests like "Give me 20 viral TikTok ideas" give ideas with no evidence behind them.

## Production

- **Volume with a quality floor (Sulfur).** One excellent video a week can lose to many adequate slideshows. Slideshows are what make steady volume possible for one person.
- **Batch production (Pedro Pérez).** He makes about 10 to 15 slideshows in one session, so the week's content is done together. The goal is a repeatable weekly system, not a day spent perfecting one post. His weekly batch:
  1. Collect five to ten recent examples that are already performing.
  2. Find the shared mechanism.
  3. Generate about 10 to 15 distinct slideshow concepts.
  4. Define the picture needed for every slide.
  5. Generate the whole asset batch in one session.
  6. Keep the visual style consistent within each slideshow.
  7. Spread the finished batch across the week.
  8. Measure results and let them shape the next batch.
- **Manual, pipeline or hybrid (Sulfur).**
  - Manual school: posts made by hand, text added in TikTok, published by hand. They value the native look and believe heavily automated posts may be detected or throttled.
  - Pipeline school: images and text variations generated in bulk, assembled by code and scheduled across many accounts. They trade some native feel for far more tests.
  - Hybrid, Sulfur's own view: automate production and organisation, not "faking humanity". Batch the assets, but keep native text, quality control and the final publish in human hands, especially on new accounts.
- **Automation removes grunt work, not judgment (Sulfur, Pedro Pérez).** Research, selection, interpretation and creative calls happen before anything is scheduled. An agent handles organising and repetitive distribution after the strategy is set.

## Images

Our own image rules live in `core/skills/image-prompt/SKILL.md` and `core/skills/style-clone/SKILL.md`; these are the articles' views.

- **Show the model a reference image (Sulfur).** A reference of the wanted style beats a written description. Generate one image at a time and give scene context step by step.
- **Real surroundings add credibility (Sulfur).** Photograph a real place and have a model put the product into it; real surroundings with generated elements feel more real than a fully generated scene.
- **Don't make everything AI (Sulfur).** Fully generated decks feel synthetic. Mix generated and real images and keep one visual tone.
- **Pinterest as a source (Pedro Pérez).** He takes most of his app-marketing images from Pinterest because they look like what a person would naturally come across and share.

## Testing and scaling

- **Search, then repeat (Sulfur).** Early on, try several formats. Once one crosses a meaningful threshold, stop searching and repeat it. If nothing works after varied tests, change the offer or the audience, not just the creative.
- **Winners become templates, losers become data (Pedro Pérez).** He repeats formats above his threshold and stops the weak ones. His own thresholds are about 10,000 to 15,000 views to repeat and under 3,000 to stop; set yours from the account's baseline and product results.
- **Treat a winner like a mining claim (Sulfur).** When a format works, make many fresh versions until results decay. Don't drop a working mechanism because it has been used once.
- **The portfolio is the machine (Sulfur).** Most posts get little reach and a few drive most of the results, so run a portfolio of posts and accounts instead of expecting every post to hit. His hit rates are anecdotal.
- **Relevance beats reach (Pounds).** A few highly targeted views can be worth more than many random ones. Judge by conversion, not vanity metrics.

## Distribution and markets

- **Start local, then go global (Sulfur).** One operator found it easier to learn and grow in his home market than through a VPN-targeted US account. Validate a format naturally first, then adapt the proven winner for harder or more valuable markets.
- **Why slideshows get distribution (Sulfur's hypothesis).** People read at their own speed, go back through slides, save useful ones and finish short sequences; he believes these are strong platform signals. This is his explanation, not TikTok documentation.
- **Text changes the viewing state (Sulfur's hypothesis).** Video puts people in an entertainment state; reading puts them in an information state, which may help a product's message land.

## Audience and funnel

- **Build an owned audience (Pounds).** Use the traffic to build email, a community or another audience you control, instead of depending on one platform. Keep giving value there and connect it to an aligned offer.
- **Problem to funnel (Pounds).** Make slides about one specific problem of one specific person, send the traffic to a free community or other owned audience about that problem, keep nurturing it, then present a relevant offer. His example is affiliate income; the lesson is that the post, the audience, the follow-up and the offer all solve the same problem.
- **Treat slides as direct response, not cheap traffic (Pounds).** He rejects "copy and spam". A slideshow should capture attention, build desire and convert a chosen audience.

## Product

- **Build for the screenshot (Sulfur).** Give the product at least one screen, widget, result or visual moment that is understood in under a second, so it can be shown inside the content format.
