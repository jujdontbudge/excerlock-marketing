# Hooks and formats

## Inputs
- The account name. Read `accounts/<name>/account.md` (audience and voice) and `sections/slideshows/accounts/<name>/README.md` (house-rule overrides).
- Either a title from the captain (job A) or a request for hook ideas (job B).

## Reference files
- `core/creative-rules.md`: the captain-only hook rule. Apply it before any hook selection; it overrides older hook-writing directions.
- `hooks.md`: the 24 hook types, with examples and why each works.
- `formats.md`: the deck formats, slide by slide, with the hook types that lead into each.
- `sections/slideshows/hook-bank.md`: the captain's own hooks, filed under the same 24 type names.
- `sections/slideshows/rules.md`: house rules, cited by ID below.
- `research/slideshow-articles.md`: the articles' business and production lessons (batching, testing, scaling). Not needed to plan a deck.

## Job A: the captain gives a title
1. Put the title on slide 1 exactly as he wrote it (H-captain-copy-final).
2. Name its hook type from `hooks.md`. If it is two types, name both and mark the one whose promise the deck has to pay (a numbered promise must pay the number; a curiosity gap must close the loop).
3. Write the title's promise in one line: what the reader expects to get by the last slide.
4. Pick the format from `formats.md` whose "Leads in from" list has that hook type and whose shape can pay the promise. Say in one line why it fits.
5. Lay the format onto our slots: H-slide-count, H-cta-last-slide, H-one-story. The slot structure and the wording of each slide belong to `sections/slideshows/skills/write-script/SKILL.md`.
6. Hand over the deck plan.

Output, the deck plan:
```
Title (slide 1, verbatim): ...
Hook type: <number and name> (second type if any)
Promise: <one line>
Format: <name>, because <one line>
Slide 1: hook. Slide 2: <job>. Slide 3: <job>. ... (one job per slide, each one paying part of the promise)
```

## Job B: select existing hooks
1. Read the captain's current message and the captain-written hook bank.
2. Select only his existing hooks, verbatim, under `core/creative-rules.md`. Do not fill gaps with new hooks or adapt third-party wording.
3. For each selected hook, give its source, type, suitable format and what slide 2 shows to pay its promise.
4. If none fits, leave a blank for the captain. The shape facts below help evaluate existing hooks, not write replacements.

## Hook shape: what our own data shows
Figures are views and saves at capture, not installs.

- **Short.** Median hook is 7 words in every view tier; the 1M+ tier runs 4 to 10 words, and 19% of its hooks are 1 to 3 words against 8% in the bottom tier. (`data/slideshow-copy-analysis/report.md` §a.1, §e.1)
- **Native text in the top third.** Plain white TikTok text on a photo: median 185,600 views, against 118,950 for designed typography. 353 of 611 hooks start in the top third of the image. (same, §b, §e.2)
- **Spoken.** Winning hooks read like a text message: dropped subjects, "gotta", "idk", "af", lowercase or casual case. Exclamation marks sit flat at 4% and add nothing. Case alone is not the lever: all-lowercase hooks have a median 120,800 views and ALL CAPS 222,950, the caps ones mostly saveable posters. (same, §b, §d, §e.6)
- **One concrete noun.** Top hooks name a thing you can picture ("rice cakes", "a man's text", "10,000 steps a day"), not a category ("wellness", "mindset"). (same, §d, §e.7)
- **An exact number.** Hooks with a number: median 166,700 views against 136,950 without. (same, §d.6, §e.8)
- **An unfinished sentence, finished by slide 2.** Ellipsis hooks: median 354,600 views against 125,800. Two-slide setup and reveal decks: median 442,550 views in the read sample. (same, §a.4, §c.1)
- **Quotes for reach, questions for comments.** Quote hooks: median 278,700 views. Question hooks: median 70,000 views but 1.55 comments per 1k, six times the median. (same, §a.5, §d.12)
- **Each engine has its own hook.** Views are led by one-word labels, quoted objections and one-line confessions; saves by lists and how-tos; comments by questions; shares by relatable confessions. (same, §a.3)
- **Show the identity, don't announce it.** "the position you'll catch us in when we're doom scrolling": 3.2M views. "for the girls who can never put their phone down": 32k. (same, §d.3)
- **No teaser to the last slide.** "last one free", "best tip at the end": 13 posts, median 16,700 views. (same, §a.9, §d.9)
- **The phone beats the word walking.** Phone and screen-time hooks: median 155,500 views, 12.8 saves per 1k. Walking and steps hooks: 80,550 views, 2.9 saves per 1k. (same, §a.10)
- **Open on a person doing the thing.** 29 of 31 organic fitness TikToks open on a person mid-action or mid-speech; none open on a logo or product. Across all 595 fitness videos, three hook grammars cover about 70%: name the pain and promise the mechanism (paid), do the thing on screen before explaining (organic), and call out the viewer conditionally ("if you're in NYC and a 30-min walk is too far..."), the best result for the effort. (`research/video-analysis-595/report.md` §3c, §6a, §6b)
- **Text on the first frame.** Burned-in text in the first one or two frames is in most fitness ads and TikToks; the few organic posts without it open on a sensory action or a face-to-camera confession. (same, §6a)

## Don'ts
From the three articles (sources in `hooks.md`). Each applies to the plan in job A and the hooks in job B.

- Don't waste slide 1. Why: attention is the first step of AIDA; if slide 1 doesn't stop her, nothing on the later slides is ever read. (Pounds)
- Don't let a slide fail to earn the next swipe, and don't label a slide ("step 2") instead of hooking it. Why: each slide's only job is to make her want the next one; a label gives no reason to keep going. (Pounds, Sulfur)
- Don't sell two desires in one deck. Why: several benefits, products or ideas blur the one thing she wants and the one thing she should do. (Pounds)
- Don't copy one post's slides; copy the recipe that several winners share. Why: one reference invites imitation, while several reveal the pattern that actually made them work. (Sulfur)
- Don't copy the topic of a winner; copy its mechanism. Why: the mechanism (suspicion, missing information, a reveal) is what made people stop, and ten decks on the same topic read as copies. (Pedro Pérez)
- Don't trust a single viral post as proof of a format. Why: one hit can be timing or luck; a format that works recently, repeatedly and across related accounts is real evidence. (Sulfur)
- Don't make it look polished or like an ad. Why: people open mail that looks personal and bin what looks like advertising; Halbert's glossy magazine barely broke even. Native, clear and simple wins. (Pounds)
- Don't aim at everyone. Why: a narrow, relatable deck draws the person who wants the product; a broad one draws views that never install. (Pounds, Sulfur)
- Don't lead with the ingredient or the mechanism. Why: she wants the transformation; "A Face Lift In a Jar" sells what a cactus-cream label can't. (Pounds)
- Don't manufacture a desire. Why: selling what she already wants (an existing pain, fear or ambition) works; creating a new want doesn't. (Pounds)
- Don't polish the wording of a weak idea. Why: research finds the angle, and clever wording can't rescue an average idea. (Pounds)
- Don't open on the product or announce it. Why: a deck that sells the problem first, and lets her notice the product, gets the "what app is that?" reaction, which is stronger intent than a pitch. (Sulfur)
- Don't pair a strong line with a generic picture. Why: the hook is the words and the image together; the emotion has to read at thumbnail size. (Sulfur)
- Don't expect music, hashtags or the caption to save a weak hook. Why: they are secondary; music should match the mood and the caption stay searchable, but neither makes up for a weak first slide. (Sulfur)
- Don't ask an AI for "20 viral ideas" from nothing. Why: ideas with no real winning posts behind them have no evidence behind them either. (Pedro Pérez)
