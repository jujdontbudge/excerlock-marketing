# Hook types

The 24 hook types used to name a slide-1 title and to write new ones. The names match the headings in `sections/slideshows/hook-bank.md`, so the captain's hooks of each type sit under the same heading there.

Each type has what it is, examples and why it works. Examples in quotes are the article authors' or real posts from our 611-slideshow hook study (`data/slideshow-copy-analysis/report.md`, views captured 14 to 18 Sep 2026). The author tag says which article the type comes from; the links are under Sources. The authors' figures are their own and were not verified.

Some types are close cousins. A title can be two types at once; name the one whose promise the deck has to pay.

## The 24 types

### 1. News
What it is: the opening says something is newly found, newly available or finally solved.
Examples: "Something new", "Scientific discovery", "At last".
Why it works: people stop for what they have not seen yet, and "finally" implies earlier attempts failed like theirs did. (Pounds)

### 2. Promise a benefit
What it is: the title says what the viewer gets by swiping through.
Example: "4 apps that will make you disgustingly productive" (1.4M views, 134,100 saves).
Why it works: the swipe has a stated payoff, so the viewer knows what she is trading her attention for. (Pounds)

### 3. Combine news and benefit
What it is: a new discovery plus the result it gives, in one line.
Example: "POV: you finally found a walking pad that doesn't make you nervous to step on" (1.5M views).
Why it works: news stops the scroll and the benefit tells her why to keep going; each half covers the other's weak spot. (Pounds)

### 4. Specificity
What it is: exact numbers or exact details instead of general words.
Example: "How to lose 13 LB in 17 days".
Why it works: an exact, odd number reads as something that really happened; "results" and "transform" read as ad copy. In our hook study, hooks with a number have a median 166,700 views against 136,950 without. (Pounds)

### 5. Warning
What it is: tells the viewer not to do something until she has done something else.
Template: "Don't do ___ until you do ___".
Why it works: it implies she is about to make a mistake, and the fix is one swipe away. (Pounds)

### 6. Secrets
What it is: promises information that seems hidden or not widely known.
Example: Pounds gives none; the common form is "what no one tells you about ___".
Why it works: being let in on something makes the viewer feel ahead of everyone else. The payoff slides must feel like insider knowledge or the hook reads as a lie. Our hook study caps the lecture version: "here's what no one tells you" style hooks all sit under 50k views. See 13 for the tone that works. (Pounds)

### 7. Numbered list
What it is: a set quantity of ways, tips or items.
Template: "17 ways to ___".
Why it works: the number tells her how much value she gets and how long it takes, before she commits. Lists are the save engine in our hook study (18.5 saves per 1k views against an 8.9 median), but not the view engine. (Pounds)

### 8. Specific-person personalization
What it is: speaks to one narrow person with one problem and one desire.
Example: "broke college girl who still has acne".
Why it works: the right viewer sees herself and stops; everyone else scrolls, which is the point. Our data sharpens it: name her by her situation, not by a label. "for the girls who..." labels have a median 91,100 views and 2.1 saves per 1k, while situational hooks ("when you're bored", "instead of doomscrolling") do far better. (Pounds)

### 9. Transformation instead of ingredient
What it is: names the outcome she already wants instead of the mechanism or ingredient that gets it.
Examples: "A Face Lift In a Jar" instead of a cactus-based cream; "Supplements that will make you feel like you're on steroids" instead of "Best supplements to take for gym beginners"; "These 3 things made me drop fat around my belly without even trying" instead of "Best ways to lose fat fast".
Why it works: the result is what she wants; the ingredient is only how. The result is also easier to picture. (Pounds)

### 10. Pattern interrupt
What it is: an unusual image, object, statement or detail on slide 1 that breaks habitual scrolling. Pounds compares it to Gary Halbert gluing a coin to a sales letter.
Examples: a strange object in frame; a line that contradicts what she believes ("STOP WALKING AN HOUR A DAY TO LOSE FAT", 354k views).
Why it works: her "wait, what?" is a swipe. In our hook study, contrarian text with no picture stalls (27.5k); contrarian text with a proof photo clears 300k. (Pounds)

### 11. Numbered promise
What it is: the first-person, already-happened version of 7: a number plus a result the narrator got.
Example: "7 things that fixed my gut".
Why it works: it is a list with proof built in. "fixed my" says it worked for a real person, so each item carries a small testimonial. (Sulfur)

### 12. Signs and symptoms
What it is: a numbered set of signs that the viewer has a problem.
Example: "3 signs your pores need a deep clean".
Why it works: she checks herself against each sign; recognising one makes the next slide personal. (Sulfur)

### 13. Secret knowledge
What it is: the tone version of 6, framed as almost forbidden.
Example: "this feels illegal to know".
Why it works: the playful "illegal" frame promises an edge without the lecture tone that sinks 6. (Sulfur)

### 14. Relatable pain
What it is: names a frustration the viewer already feels, in her own words.
Example: "why does my flat look like a student loan".
Why it works: recognition first, solution later. The phone confession is the live version for us: phone and screen-time hooks in our hook study have a median 155,500 views and 12.8 saves per 1k, against 80,550 and 2.9 for hooks that lead with walking. (Sulfur)

### 15. Another person, conflict or doubt, then a changed reaction
What it is: someone else doubts, forbids or questions, and the deck shows what changed their reaction.
Examples: "my landlord said i can't change anything so i showed her what AI thinks it could look like"; "i showed my mum what AI thinks our living room could be"; "babe why do you have an umbrella in your car".
Why it works: a second person brings story and tension before the result appears, which Sulfur rates stronger than opening on the product. The reader takes a side and swipes to see the reaction. Quoted speech does the same job in our hook study: quote hooks have a median 278,700 views. (Sulfur)

### 16. Curiosity gap
What it is: opens a loop that only the swipe can close.
Examples: "we were together six months, then he sent me this"; "i found out what he'd been hiding".
Why it works: not swiping leaves an itch. In our hook study the unfinished sentence is the most reliable swipe device: ellipsis hooks have a median 354,600 views against 125,800 without, and slide 2 usually finishes the sentence with a picture. It drives swipes, not saves. (Sulfur)

### 17. Borrowed credibility
What it is: an open loop plus proof, status or a result that gives her a reason to believe.
Examples: "extremely chic things my twin sister does"; "how i kept a 4.5 gpa with adhd".
Why it works: the credential answers "why listen to you" before she asks, and the hard condition ("with adhd") makes the result feel earned. (Sulfur)

### 18. Cultural mystery
What it is: inside information from a distant, unfamiliar or hidden world.
Examples: "gym rules in japan that will shock westerners"; "secret codes at chinese business lunches that decide if you get the deal".
Why it works: curiosity about how other people live, plus the promise of a surprise ("will shock"). (Sulfur)

### 19. Information as the value
What it is: the usefulness of the information is the whole reason to swipe.
Examples: "top 10 degrees that make you rich"; "my clean iphone setup".
Why it works: she swipes to own something useful, and saves it to use later. Suspense does less of the work, so every slide after the hook must deliver real substance. (Sulfur)

### 20. Repeatable signature
What it is: one recognisable opening line reused on every post until it becomes the account's identity.
Example: "hey, i'm joe, you doomscrolled your way to my art, enjoy".
Why it works: returning viewers recognise it at once, and the repetition builds a series she expects to continue. (Sulfur)

### 21. Engagement bait
What it is: a question asked mainly to get comments.
Example: asking viewers what they would put in an empty room.
Why it works: an easy, opinion-shaped question gets answered by everyone with an opinion. In our hook study, question hooks earn 1.55 comments per 1k views, six times the median, but only a median 67,000 views, so use it when comments are the goal. (Sulfur)

### 22. Image-led hook
What it is: the picture on slide 1 does the hooking and the words only name it. Sulfur's point is that the hook is the words and the image together.
Example: the same story got far better results when a generic image was swapped for a close-up crying face.
Why it works: emotion has to read at thumbnail size, and the pain or the result should be visible at once. In our hook study, when the picture is the hook, one or two words are enough ("Pasta", "oats": median 998k views for one-word label hooks). (Sulfur)

### 23. Every-slide hook
What it is: every slide after the first carries its own small hook instead of a label.
Example: "step 2: the trick that feels harder but works 3x faster" instead of "step 2".
Why it works: a label gives no reason to keep going; a line that promises a stronger or stranger result keeps the loop open slide after slide. (Sulfur)

### 24. Reuse a psychological mechanism in a new scenario
What it is: keep the psychological engine of a proven hook and move it to a new setting.
Examples, from a suspected-cheating story whose engine is suspicion, missing information, then a reveal: "I thought my best friend was hiding something from me. Then I saw this photo."; "My boss called me into his office after work. I had no idea why until he showed me this."; "My girlfriend told me she was going to sleep early. At 2:14 AM, I received this message."
Why it works: the engine is what made people stop; the topic was only its costume. Moving the engine gives fresh posts that are already tested. (Pedro Pérez)

## Taking a reference hook apart

When studying a post that worked, record these for it (Pedro Pérez's checklist), then name its hook type from the list above:

- the first one or two seconds, the hook text and the first image;
- the story, the curiosity mechanism and the emotional trigger;
- the visual progression, the amount of text and the pacing;
- the payoff and the call to action;
- the most-liked comments.
