# Write a slideshow script

## The goal

A girl scrolling TikTok stops on the hook, swipes because every slide earns it, and leaves feeling she found an app, not that she was sold one. That is direct response done quietly: attention, desire, and the product as the obvious next step.

So:
- **It must look organic.** It sits unnoticed among the real top decks in her feed: their words, their photos, their length (`H-organic-feed`).
- **Every non-CTA slide earns its place** by entertaining her or giving her something she can use. If a slide does neither, cut it.
- **The CTA is quiet but useful.** It solves the problem the deck is about, like a friend mentioning what worked. She should feel she discovered it, or that she needs it.

## How

1. Require an explicit target audience in the writing brief (`accounts/<name>/account.md`, `research/README.md`) before selecting a topic. Run a topic-to-audience fit check BEFORE drafting: broadly inspect the organic corpus (`research/README.md` and `research/tagged-hooks/hooks.md`) to confirm that the specific problem or desire actually recurs in unpromoted posts for that audience, distinguishing source evidence from inference. The source post or skeleton provides the structure or format, not the default topic; never reuse an unrelated topic or invent an ungrounded premise. Know why that hook stops the scroll for that demographic and what it promises; the deck exists to pay that promise.
Before drafting, apply the premise-and-payoff reasoning below. Topic relevance alone is not enough.

2. Follow the corpus-first writing procedure below before composing slide text. Audience labels guide retrieval; the actual source captions supply viewer-facing language.
3. Write one story she can follow slide by slide (`H-one-story`), only as long as it needs (`H-slide-count`), the app as one of her tips on any value slide and "GO GET THIS" as the last slide (`H-cta-last-slide`). Pull the value slides from different parts of her life (food, sleep, errands, gym, routine), not five versions of the same tip. Why: the winning decks are diverse; five walking tips in a row reads as one tip repeated.
4. Read it back as the scroller. Cut anything that sounds written instead of said, any slide that repeats another, anything that feels like a pitch.

Tools, never a template:
- **Proof early** (a receipt from `sections/slideshows/assets.md`, a before picture). Use it when the hook makes a claim she'd doubt; skip it when the story doesn't need it.
- **A before and after**, when the hook is a change story.
- **A payoff before the final CTA**, when it adds something. The ending follows `H-cta-last-slide` and any explicit captain-approved exception.

## Corpus-first writing: select, adapt, connect

**Goal:** build a coherent slideshow from demonstrated organic hooks, ideas and language, rather than inventing copy and finding vaguely related sources afterward. This procedure also governs the language guidance in skeletons.

**Keep two vocabularies separate.** The captain's niche descriptions and our research labels are internal targeting language, not captions. 'Fitness girls' describes whom to research; it does not establish that creators call themselves that or say 'making fitness harder.' Use actual viewer-facing wording found in source posts. Do not infer organic phrasing from the brief, or claim nobody uses a phrase without checking the corpus.

1. **Retrieve the captain's hook before writing the deck.** Apply `core/creative-rules.md`: use only a hook he wrote, verbatim. Research can inform the content and format, but third-party hooks are not approved replacements. If none fits, leave the hook blank for him rather than inventing or adapting one.
2. **Search the full slide text across the available bank.** Use the indexes to locate collected slideshow captions and video transcripts, not only titles or previously selected examples. Scan the available text broadly, then read the full relevant posts in context. Keep a brief record of which collections were searched and which text was unavailable; never claim to have scanned all posts after reading a handful. Retrieve candidate material before choosing the final slide contents.
3. **Select source-supported content that answers this hook.** Each substantive sign or tip needs a specific source passage, not a related hook or a broad theme such as consistency. Preserve the original idea, qualifications and conversational phrasing. Adapt person, tense and connective wording as needed so it belongs in this deck. Don't invent a relatable-sounding behavior to fill a slot. If the bank cannot supply enough distinct fitting items, change the candidate hook or report insufficient material.
4. **Read the hook followed immediately by each slide.** Maintain the hook's point of view and the kind of answer it calls for. A personal signs hook should address the viewer with observations such as 'you skip...' or 'your...' rather than silently switching into detached activity labels such as 'skipping...'. Short fragments are acceptable when they naturally complete the promise; the rule is linguistic and semantic continuity, not adding 'you' mechanically to every format. For skeletons, describe this relationship explicitly without imposing identical sentence construction on all slides.
5. **Keep a separate source trace.** Record the original hook, source IDs/paths, the supporting passage for each chosen item and what was adapted in an evidence note beside the script. Keep citations and analysis out of the script itself. The trace must show where the actual idea and wording came from; plausible agent explanations do not substitute for sources.

**Required output:** the normal script plus its separate evidence note. Completion means every proposed hook and substantive slide has been traced to inspected corpus text, fits the promise in meaning and grammar, and adds a distinct reason to continue. The captain's examples explain the desired reasoning; they are not assigned copy. Selection, arrangement and careful adaptation are the creative work here—not inventing unsupported hooks and tips.

## Premise and payoff: understand the meaning, not just the template

**Goal:** a viewer has a meaningful reason to start and continue. The skeleton preserves a way of engaging her, not a sentence whose nouns can be swapped. Use agent judgment; these are reasoning obligations, not a scoring formula.

- **Understand the actual question or promise.** State privately what the viewer wants to discover and what would genuinely answer it. Don't invent uncertainty around something she can directly observe merely to fill a signs-list format. A related keyword or a high-view source does not make the new premise meaningful.
- **Choose within the audience, not a compulsory product topic.** Fitness girls have interests in training, appearance, progress, food, movement and everyday life. Walking is available, not mandatory. Use the brief's audience and organic evidence to find a compelling concern or possibility; never force every format to be about steps.
- **Require relevance of evidence, not just subject matter.** For each slide, explain in ordinary language how the observation supports the hook's exact promise. If the explanation is strained, rests on an unsupported causal claim, or merely says both are fitness-related, reject the slide. Don't confuse a symptom, a cause, an opportunity and a measurement.
- **Answering the hook and earning a swipe are separate requirements.** A relevant but obvious line can still be filler. Each slide should offer a worthwhile discovery, specific recognition, explanation, motivation, entertainment or unexpected possibility. Name the actual payoff in the line; labels such as 'relatable' or 'curiosity-driven' are not evidence of one. Novelty need not mean a never-before-seen fact: delivery and a sharply recognised experience can earn attention too.
- **For signs lists, look for something the viewer may not recognise about her situation.** It might be an unwanted outcome or condition, an explanation of an unwanted experience, or an unrealised opportunity for a wanted outcome. These are possibilities, not mandatory templates. The observations must reveal that particular thing, not pad the required count.
- **Reject a bad fit before drafting.** Don't rationalise a weak premise because a skeleton and audience have already been selected. Research another topic; if none fits convincingly, explain the mismatch rather than manufacturing signs. Organic evidence should support the actual concern and angle, not merely the broad category.

### Contrast examples: reasoning demonstrations, not suggested scripts

'How to know your daily steps are too low' was rejected because it manufactures a question about a directly checkable count. 'Feeling burned out at 3pm' does not establish that count and therefore does not answer the hook. Adding six more loosely related complaints does not create a discovery.

'Signs you could be getting more steps' changes the promise to overlooked opportunities. 'You sit through every phone call' can support that promise by identifying an occasion when movement may be possible; it does not prove the current count is too low. This is a logical-fit example, not automatic approval of an obvious line or that topic.

'Signs you could be burning more calories' similarly opens the possibility of unused opportunities rather than diagnosing a measured total. It still needs credible, relevant and engaging observations. A better title does not rescue unrelated symptoms. These alternatives illustrate the captain's distinction; don't copy them as the next assignment or make walking compulsory.

## Output

`sections/slideshows/accounts/<name>/decks/NN-slug/script.md`, nothing else in it:

```
# <hook>

1. <on-screen text>
   Picture: <one line: what the photo shows>
2. ...
```

The last slide's picture gets the App Store bar (`H-cta-last-slide`). Organic references are found later, at the image step.

## Don'ts

The house rules in `sections/slideshows/rules.md` also apply; they are not repeated here.

- Don't fill a fixed slide recipe (hook, proof, tip, CTA, tip, tip). Why: every deck then reads the same, and the captain rejected that as formulaic (2026-09-24). The story decides the slides.
- Preserve source-supported conversational language rather than replacing it with invented 'organic' phrasing. Use the corpus-first procedure above: adapt minimally for a coherent new deck, respect quotation limits, and avoid assembling unrelated phrases that do not answer the hook.
- Don't put anything in the script file beyond slide text and picture lines: no citations, self-checks, summaries or labels. Why: the captain reads the deck, not the paperwork.
- Retrieve exact captain wording from `core/approved-hooks.md` or the current message. Cleaned historical banks and source-format templates are not permission to rewrite a hook. Why: an agent rewrote his quoted hook and added a bracketed tail (captain, 2026-10-05).
- Don't write any tip about watching, scrolling or listening to your phone or TV while walking (walking-workout videos, a show on the walking pad, scrolling TikTok while pacing). Only the CTA may pair screens with moving. Also cut any tip so complete that it solves the problem the app solves. Why: the Netflix walking-workout tip is exactly what ExcerLock does, and it pulls attention away from the CTA (captain, 2026-10-05).
- Don't write a tip that gates an activity behind walking ("only listen to a podcast while walking", "only watch shows on the walking pad"). "Take a class" or "walk while you do X" is fine. Only the CTA and ExcerLock may say something is allowed only while moving. Why: that gate is ExcerLock's own feature, and a free tip that does it steals the product's shine (captain, 2026-09-24).
- Don't write the CTA as ad copy or as "i use excerlock". Why: a product slide written like the free slides saves at 95.8 per 1k while slogan wording saves at 0.6 to 1.2.
- Don't put a statistic or a mood line on a value slide. Why: she came for something she can use; mood slides save at 7.4 per 1k against 36.5 for light tips.
- Don't write parentheticals in on-screen text unless the captain wrote them. Why: agent-written brackets read as AI.
- Don't make every slide the same length and shape. Why: a deck that mixes short and long slides gets both views and saves.
- Plan pictures under `H-gym-pictures`, `H-house-look`, `H-one-look` and `H-no-watch`, including explicit account overrides. A picture belongs to the deck's fitness world; it need not literally illustrate the tip. The superseded two-face limit does not override the current gym-picture mix.
